Irenaeus and the author of John’s Gospel

NT criticism
Author

Matthew Brett

Published

August 1, 2026

The central question

Church tradition has the apostle John as the author of the Gospel according to John, and the three letters of John (1 John, 2 John, 3 John).

By “the apostle John” I mean John, son of Zebedee, brother of James, one of the twelve named apostles in the Gospels. At the beginning of e.g. Mark (Mark 1:16-20), he is a fisherman, working in a business with his brother and father:

Now walking beside the sea of Galilee, he saw Simon and Andrew his brother, sons of Simon, casting a net in the sea; for they were fishermen. And Jesus said to them, Come-here after me and I will make you to become fishermen of men. And immediately they left their nets and followed him. And he advanced a few feet from there and saw James and John his brother, the sons of Zebedee, and they were in the ship mending the nets. And immediately he called them, and they left their father Zebedee with the hired-hands in the ship and went after him. (Modern Literal Version - MLV)1

For reasons that will soon become clear, I will call this person “John son of Zebedee” or JsZ for short.

For the moment, I’ll assume that there is a single primary author of John’s Gospel. Let’s say we don’t know who that author is. So we’ll call this person the “Author of John’s Gospel” or AJG for short.

For reasons I won’t detail here, but discussed in Lightfoot (1904) (chapter I, p3) and most famously described in (dodd1963historical?), there is internal evidence that AJG knew geographical and historical details of first-century Palestine and Jerusalem and Judaism, that make it tempting to assume that AJG was an eye-witness. JsZ would have been such an eye-witness. But was he the author of John?

The Gospel itself indicates that it was written by “the disciple whom Jesus loved”, otherwise written as “the beloved disciple”. From John 21:21-22,24:

But Peter turned again and sees the disciple whom Jesus loved following; who also leaned-back upon his chest at the supper and who had said, Lord, who is he who is giving you up? … This is the disciple who is testifying concerning these things and wrote these things and we know that his testimony is true.

For various reasons, it seems likely that we are to understand it was this disciple who was the “other disciple” that went with Peter to the house of the High Priest while Jesus was being tried (John 18:15-16):

Now Simon Peter was following Jesus and the other disciple. Now that disciple was known to the high-priest and entered in together-with Jesus into the courtyard of the high-priest; but Peter stood outside before the door. Therefore, the other disciple, who was known to the high-priest, went forth and spoke to the doorkeeper and led in Peter. (MLV)

It’s possible, but hard, to see how a fisherman, even someone who worked in a business with hired hands, would have been “known to the high-priest”.

One response to this and other difficulties, is to suggest that John, the beloved disciple, was a disciple named John, other than JsZ. Martin Hengel made this proposal in Hengel (1989), followed by Bauckham (1993) and others.

However, there is a hard test for this hypothesis, in the form of Irenaeus of Lyons. He wrote several works in the late second century, listed below, that cite John and refer to the authorship of the Gospel and letters. He would have had good reason to know who had written the Gospel and letters, because he had been a disciple of Polycarp (c69 AD - c155 AD), who had in turn been taught by a John who had seen Jesus. Irenaeus writes, in IF II (letter to Florinus):

I can even describe the place where the blessed Polycarp used to sit and discourse — his going out, too, and his coming in — his general mode of life and personal appearance, together with the discourses which he delivered to the people; also how he would speak of his familiar intercourse with John, and with the rest of those who had seen the Lord; and how he would call their words to remembrance.

As we will see, it must be this John that Irenaeus believes to be AJG.

So, there is a strong eye-witness tradition linking AJG to Irenaeus, through Polycarp, and perhaps through Pothinus, Irenaeus’ predecessor as Bishop of Lyons, who may also have been a disciple of Polycarp.

The test for the Hengel / Bauckham hypothesis, is whether Irenaeus’ substantial extant writings can reasonably be read on the understanding that Irenaeus knew that AJG was not JsZ, but another disciple of Jesus, also called John.

That is the theme for the rest of the page, starting with some discussion and summary, and ending with all evidence I could bring to bear from Irenaeus’ writing.

As J B. Lightfoot points out, it is worth reading Irenaeus directly on his testimony to the four Gospels, and especially John:

If any reader is really in earnest on [the four-Gospel Canon], I will ask him to read Irenaeus and judge for himself. He will find many things for which perhaps he is not prepared, and which will jar with his preconceived ideas; but on the one point at issue I have no fear that I shall be accused of exaggeration. Indeed it is impossible to convey in a few paragraphs the whole force of an impression which is deepened by each successive page of a long and elaborate work. (Lightfoot 1889, p271)

To anticipate, it does seem to me that Irenaeus’ writing is compatible with AJG as JsZ, or with AJG being another of Jesus’ disciples, also called John. It’s striking that AJG in Irenaeus is very specifically and often called “the disciple of the Lord”, which seems to have been a familiar title of AJG, and no other disciple. Perhaps this suggests “the disciple whom Jesus loved” of the Gospel text.

Irenaeus and his texts

Irenaeus’ life and writing are summarized in:

  • Wikipedia on Irenaeus
  • Britannica on Irenaeus
  • Development of the NT Canon: Irenaeus

His known works are: (see Britannica):

  • Against heresies (AH)
  • Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching (DAP)
  • Various fragments.

Eusebius of Caesarea quotes from, and refers to Irenaeus’ work in his Ecclesiastical History (EH) — 316-326 AD (also see J. B. Lightfoot’s biography of Eusebius (1880)).

Use of John and “apostle” in Irenaeus’ writing

Most of the quotes here are from Irenaeus’ five books of Against Heresies — abbreviated AH, but I also cover Irenaeus’ other known work, as you’ll see below.

The quotes below record all mentions of “John” and “apostle” in Irenaeus, to document who Irenaeus thinks John is, and how Irenaeus uses the term “apostle”, and whether, and in what sense, Irenaeus considered John to be an apostle.

Sources

Roberts and Donaldson (1885a) has the full text in English translation. Harvey (1857) is an edition of the Latin text that is our main source for AH. I refer to the AH text below by book number, chapter number (in Roman numerals) and section number — e.g AH 4, XXXV:2. Summary of results

John the disciple of the Lord

When referring to John the author, Irenaeus very often uses the set phrase “John, the disciple of the Lord” (14 times of 49 total uses of “John” other than John the Baptist). There are two places where he indicates John by “disciple of the lord”, or “His disciple”, without naming him, although John is clear from the context: AH 3, XI:3, AH 3, XVI:8. He does not use the phrase “disciple of the Lord” of any other person, though he several times refers in general to the “disciples of the Lord” or “His disciples” in AH 2, II:5; AH 3, XII:4-5 (twice); AH 3, XII:14; AH 3, XVI:5; AH 5, XXXIV:3.

So “disciple of [the,our] Lord”, or “the Lord’s disciple” in Irenaeus always means John the author of the Gospel or the letters, or the Apocalypse.

This is similar to the phrasing of Papias, quoted by Eusebius in Eusebius of Caesarea (1890) (EH III. 39, p289). From Lightfoot and Harmer (1891), p529, (quoting Eusebius, quoting Papias):

And again, any occasion when a person came (in my way) who had been a follower of the Elders, I would inquire about the discourses of the elders — what was said by Andrew, or by Peter, or by Philip, or by Thomas or James, or by John or Matthew or any other of the Lord’s disciples, and what Aristion and the Elder John, the disciples of the Lord, say.

John’s Gospel gives its own primary author as “the beloved disciple” (John 21:24), who is not otherwise explicitly named or identified.

The author of the second and third letters of John introduces himself only as “The elder” (2 John 1:1, 3 John 1:1).

Revelation (the Apocalypse) names its source as “[Jesus Christ’s] servant John” (Revelation 1:1)

Irenaeus’ apostles

Irenaeus only ever uses “the Apostle N” or “N the Apostle” for: Paul (17 times); Matthew (once of 17 mentions of “Matthew”) and Peter (once for 48 mentions of “Peter”). He never uses “the Apostle John” or “John the Apostle”.

For Irenaeus, the most representative apostles are Peter and Paul — he criticizes the heretics for claiming to be “superior to his disciples, such as Peter and Paul, and the rest of the apostles” AH 1, XXV:2, and contrasts the heretics’ teaching with that of the apostles with “Therefore let them not any longer assert that Peter and Paul and the other apostles proclaimed the truth …” AH 4, XXXV:2.

In his letters, Paul specifically and often identifies himself as an “apostle of Christ Jesus” (e.g. Romans 1:1, 1 and 2 Corinthians 1), but did not claim to have met Jesus in person.

When referring to apostles, Irenaeus sometimes means:

  1. The 12 named apostles; I’ve labeled these references as “12” below.
  2. Paul
  3. The source of definitive teaching from and about Jesus. I’ve labeled these references as “SA”.

The question at hand is whether Irenaeus, when citing the apostles as general authority (SA), restricted the apostles to the 12 plus Paul.

As background, it is clear from the quotes below that:

  1. Irenaeus identifies the author of John’s Gospel as John, the disciple of the Lord: “… John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia.” AH 3, I:1. Also: “And to these things does John also, the disciple of the Lord, bear witness, when he speaks thus in the Gospel: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. …” AH 5, XVIII:2.
  2. The same John, disciple of the Lord, is the author of the Apocalypse: “And if any one will devote a close attention to those things … which John the disciple of the Lord saw in the Apocalypse,” … AH 4, XXX:4.
  3. The same John is the author of letters 1 John and 2 John. AH 1, XVI:3 has “John, the disciple of the Lord … says …” followed by a quote from 2 John. In AH 3, XVI:8: “His disciple, in his Epistle already mentioned,” (quoting 2 John), and later: “Wherefore he again exclaims in his Epistle,” (quoting 1 John).
  4. Irenaeus considered John the disciple of the Lord to be an apostle: “[the heretics assert that John] has, by thus speaking, referred to the primary Ogdoad,2 in which there was as yet no Jesus, and no Christ, the teacher of John. But that the apostle did not speak concerning their conjunctions, but concerning our Lord Jesus Christ, whom he also acknowledges as the Word of God, he himself has made evident. For, summing up his statements respecting the Word previously mentioned by him, he further declares,”And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.” (AH 1, IX:2-3).

The question though, is whether Irenaeus is asserting that John — the disciple of the Lord — was also one of the 12. Could he, instead, have been referring to some other John, who was an apostle in the sense of being one of Jesus’ disciples, and therefore, a source of reliable testimony?

Irenaeus does use this second, more general interpretation of apostle, when he argues that John the Baptist could be considered an apostle, because “he actually saw [[Jesus]] when he came, and pointed him out”:

Wherefore the Lord said that He deemed [[John the Baptist]] “more than a prophet.” For all the other prophets preached the advent of the paternal Light, and desired to be worthy of seeing Him whom they preached; but John did both announce [the advent] beforehand, in a like manner as did the others, and actually saw Him when He came, and pointed Him out, and persuaded many to believe on Him, so that he did himself hold the place of both prophet and apostle. For this is to be more than a prophet, because, “first apostles, secondarily prophets;” … AH 3, XI:4.

There is another piece of suggestive evidence that Irenaeus allows other disciples of Jesus to be apostles. He writes: “For that Judas the traitor is the twelfth in order, is agreed upon by all, there being twelve apostles mentioned by name in the Gospel.” AH 2, XX:4; “mentioned by name” weakly implies there may be other apostles not mentioned by name.

Use of “apostle” in early church writing

What did other early church writers mean by the term “apostle”?

First note that Acts 14:14 (MLV) describes Barnabas as an apostle:

But after the apostles, Barnabas and Paul, heard it, they ripped their garments and dashed into the crowd, …”

Barnabas was of course not one of the 12, but was a member of the early church. He may have been an eyewitness to at least some of Jesus’ life and teaching. We first see him in Acts 4:36-37 (MLV):

Now Joses, who was surnamed Barnabas from the apostles (which is, being translated, Son of Encouragement), a Levite from Cyprus by birth, sold a field, possessed by him, and brought the money and placed it beside the apostles’ feet.

See the Lampe (1961) lexicon entry for αποστολος (emissary, apostle) (pp. 211 - 214). It lists five broad categories, of which I is “in gen”, II is “of Christ as sent forth from the Father”, and III is “of one sent by God or Christ to preach and act on his behalf;”. Sub-meaning B of III is “of NT apostles, comprising 1. The Twelve, … 2. other leaders of primitive Church …”, with various entries therein.

From Lampe we see that Clement of Alexandria, writing soon after AH, refers to Clement of Rome (died around 100) as “the apostle Clement” (Stromata 4, XVII:105) (Roberts and Donaldson 1885b, 428).

In the early fourth century, Eusebius’ Ecclesiastical History (EH), shows a wide view of the term “apostle”. From Eusebius of Caesarea (1890):

Afterwards he says he appeared unto James, who was one of the so-called brethren of the Savior. But, since in addition to these, there were many others who were called apostles, in imitation of the Twelve, as was Paul himself, he adds: “Afterward he appeared to all the apostles.” [[1 Corinthians 15:7]] EH 1, XII:4 Another version at BKV
