I have found a new use for AI: as a tool to identify what motivated reasoning would look like.

I don’t think it would be useful to ask AI whether a particular argument is best explained by motivated reasoning. But you can ask AI what an argument from motivated reasoning would look like, and compare the result to arguments that have been made.

Here’s an example from biblical scholarship.

The problem

I was listening to Mark Goodacre’s NT pod interview with Robyn Walsh.

Walsh was discussing her 2021 book The Origins of Early Christian Literature in which she proposes that the Synoptic Gospels (Mark, Matthew, Luke) should best be seen as instances of classical Greek literary works by “elite cultural producers”.

The thesis seemed extraordinarily anti-historical to me, and to probe my instincts on this, I turned to AI.

AI as diagnostic tool for motivated reasoning

You can see the whole conversation with Gemini here.

I started thus:

Let us say that I was scholar of classics, trained in classical Greek literature, and I wanted to generate a model of the early church and the influence of Jesus, that was dominated by factors within my educational expertise. What kind of model would you recommend?

You can see the response and the subsequent conversation in the full log linked above. I responded in the conversation, by offering my worries, as a classical scholar, that others might object to my thesis on various grounds. Later in the conversation:

I’m still worried though. However characterized, we have, surely, a quite unprecedented situation here, where a literary movement goes from invisible, to threatening Roman civic society, within roughly 40 years. I have to posit that Paul was - contrary to his claims - a first mover in a movement that was primarily literary, and that Mark, soon afterwards, produced a fully-formed, elaborate bios [Greek literary biography], followed by other major literary successes, such as the other Gospels and letters — but where — for some reason — this movement was — at the time, by its critics, specifically identified as being not-literate. Isn’t that going to be too much of a stretch?

To which the AI rather reasonably responded:

You are right to feel uneasy—because as you’ve laid it out, that thesis has backed itself into a logical corner.

To make that specific version of the model work, you would have to maintain four historically contradictory premises simultaneously:

  • The “Paul as First-Mover” Paradox: You must claim Paul secretly invented a literary movement from scratch, while ignoring his own explicit, earliest written testimony (Galatians 1–2, 1 Corinthians 15:3) that he was actually joining, persecuting, and then inheriting an already existing network centered in Jerusalem and Damascus.
  • The “Instant Masterpiece” Paradox: You must claim that Mark—writing in lower-register, fast-paced Koine Greek—spontaneously produced a fully formed, universally captivating, multi-layered bios out of thin air in under a decade, without any substantial prior narrative tradition or structural precedent.
  • The “Invisible High-Literary Revolution” Paradox: You must explain how an exclusively literary movement achieved explosive Mediterranean-wide growth among non-elite masses faster than almost any other movement in antiquity, while somehow leaving no trace of the aristocratic, highly educated literary salons that typically produce and consume such works.
  • The “Blind Critics” Paradox: You must explain why sophisticated 2nd-century pagan opponents (like Celsus, Lucian, and Porphyry)—who were trained in classical literary criticism and eager to dismantle the movement—unanimously characterized Christians not as a rival literary movement, but as an uneducated, text-obsessed cult of “cobblers, weavers, and slaves.”

It then continues its attempt to rescue the premise. Read the full log to see if you find that attempt convincing.
