Who did Eusebius think was the author of John’s Gospel?

Eusebius of Caesarea(c260-339) wrote, among other works, a history of the church until that point, usually called the Ecclesiastical History (EH) — 316-326 AD.

See (lightfoot1880eusebius?) for more detail on Eusebius’ life and work.

Here we are interested in who Eusebius believed had written John’s Gospel.

Part of our interest is that Eusebius quotes from the very early five books of Papias of Hierapolis called “Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord”. (carlson2025foucault_papias?) has: (footnote 48) “… Call this ESL. The consensus of Papias scholarship has settled on dating him around 110 CE …” citing p1 n.7 of his own book on the Papias fragments — (carlson2021papias?) — to which I do not have access. Papias lived in Hierapolis, which is about 200 km from Ephesus, the city in which John’s Gospel is most likely to have been written, and about 220 km from Smyrna, the home of Polycarp and the younger Irenaeus.

Lightfoot (1889) has a chapter on Papias, starting at page 142. He sets out the striking lack of good information we have for definite dates of Papias’ life, or ESL.

Although Eusebius quotes from ESL, it is not clear that Eusebius had access to any of Papias’ five books — he may have been quoting from a secondary source — see (stevens2019eusebius_papias?).

These are the relevant passages from EH on the authorship of the Gospels, and particularly, that of John. Quotes and page numbers are from Eusebius of Caesarea (1890).

Key passages from HE

HE 3.39.14b–15

… We now need to add to these statements of his [Papias’s] a tradition which he set forth about Mark who wrote the gospel as follows: “And this is what the Elder would say: ‘Mark, who had been Peter’s interpreter, accurately wrote what he remembered, yet not in order the things either said or done by the Lord.’ For he neither heard the Lord nor followed him, but later, as I said, Peter, who would give his teachings as needed, but not, as it were, making a compilation of the dominical oracles, so that Mark did not fail at all by writing some of them as he recalled. For he took care of one thing, to omit nothing of what he heard or falsify anything among them.

  1. Ecclesiastical History 3.24 (especially 3.24.1–13) Eusebius gives his own summary of the order and authorship of the four Gospels. He states that only Matthew and John among the disciples left written records, and that John wrote last:

After Mark and Luke had already published their Gospels, “they say that John, who had employed all his time in proclaiming the Gospel orally, finally proceeded to write.” The three earlier Gospels had come into his hands; he accepted them as true but noted that they lacked an account of the deeds done by Christ at the beginning of his ministry. John therefore wrote to supply what was missing (especially events before the imprisonment of John the Baptist). Eusebius calls it a “spiritual Gospel” and notes that John began with the doctrine of Christ’s divinity.

Exact reference: Historia Ecclesiastica 3.24.1–13 (especially §§5–13). 2. Ecclesiastical History 6.14.5–7 Eusebius quotes a tradition from Clement of Alexandria (Hypotyposes): “But, last of all, John, perceiving that the external facts had been made plain in the Gospel, being urged by his friends, and inspired by the Spirit, composed a spiritual Gospel.” Exact reference: Historia Ecclesiastica 6.14.5–7. 3. Ecclesiastical History 3.39.4–6 (discussion of Papias) Eusebius quotes Papias and then comments that Papias mentions two men named John. He identifies the first (listed with the apostles) as “clearly meaning the Evangelist” (i.e., the author of the Gospel), while suggesting the second (“the elder John”) may have been the author of Revelation. Exact reference: Historia Ecclesiastica 3.39.4–6.
