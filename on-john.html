On John

For a long time, I have been — what is the word? — devoted to John’s Gospel, and John’s letters.

There is something mesmerizing about them. I see it — feel it — at once with the start of John’s Gospel:

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.

He was with God in the beginning.

Through him all things were made; without him nothing was made that has been made.

In him was life, and that life was the light of all mankind.

The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.

There is architecture there. You have to look up, in awe. It is as W. H. Auden said of his aim in writing poetry, that he should say something new, but so that those who read it would think, that it has always been true.

It is as if you have entered a room, to find an old man, half in prayer, half teaching, talking about something wonderful — almost talking to himself, but talking out into history. You can almost hear him saying, perhaps muttering (John 1:1):

En archē ēn ho logos, kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon, kai theos ēn ho logos. …

And you can also feel — this man loves, worships, his friend, who spoke so fully, who lived as he did. A few verses on at the start of the Gospel:

The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us. We have seen his glory, the glory of the one and only Son, who came from the Father, full of grace and truth.

The letters read like the same old man — “the elder” — as he talks, astonished, about what he has seen (first letter):

We are writing to you about something which has always existed yet which we ourselves actually saw and heard: something which we had an opportunity to observe closely and even to hold in our hands, and yet, as we know now, was something of the very Word of life himself! For it was life which appeared before us: we saw it, we are eye-witnesses of it, and are now writing to you about it. It was the very life of all ages, the life that has always existed with the Father, which actually became visible in person to us mortal men. We repeat, we really saw and heard what we are now writing to you about.

That’s how I read it. It’s almost like music, like a long, beautiful song, full of structure and detail, but yet, nothing could be different; once it is written, we see it was always true.

That’s how I read it, but then I read commentators, and historians, and they say — well, he can’t be an eye-witness, why does Jesus speak in this way, it doesn’t sound like the Jesus of the synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke), how did a fisherman learn to write Greek like that, where do they come from, all these long speeches by Jesus, surely he didn’t remember them?

And for many of those commentators, the answer is — it’s just some person, we know not who, who made it up — it’s a fantasy — a fake; it’s a story, maybe a lovely story, but nothing but.

I was mystified by that conclusion. The Gospel just doesn’t read that way — in part because it is just so astonishing, unlike anything else in the world before and during and after the New Testament. What kind of mind was this, that could make that up? And how, in God’s name, did the church and every scholar since that time, lose track of this writer, perhaps the most gifted of all antiquity? Or, it is really true, as some have said, that there was some set of people, who all learned to talk this way, and wrote in John’s name — although, strikingly, he is not named in the Gospel or the letters.

I asked — could it really be true, that these were fakes?

I hadn’t read anything like the Gospel or letters of John, but perhaps there were others who wrote that way. So I went reading, and no, there are no others who wrote that way. I’ve read quite a lot of early church writing now — some of it is boring, some it is silly and credulous, some of it is moving and impressive, but there is nothing anything like the Gospel or the letters.

And I began to wonder, as you can see in some of my posts: is this — the historical study of early church writing — just another field that has fallen into irrelevance with capture by the need for cleverness, for ostentatious learning, for novelty — for fascinating speculations? Has that study wandered into some faint and fantasy realm where they just cannot see — as Lightfoot puts it above — the “historical position in all its bearings”.

Now I read, and read, and forget, trying to keep track of what has happened, what has been said, analysing arguments to find those that are foolish, speculative — and those that are sound, and must be answered.

Of course it’s hard. As Lightfoot says, many, perhaps not most of the people writing about these matters, do have good arguments, that must be answered. But many, perhaps most, perhaps the same people, make assertions that are far too strong, or must be wrong. It is rocky, uneven ground, and full of houses set up, taken down, in ruins, or ruined and rebuilt.

In this strange landscape, I wonder — who did write John? The writer writes very simple Greek, as you can feel in the English — with simpler syntax and a smaller vocabulary than the other Gospel writers. He knows many things about the world of the Gospel that an eye-witness would know, and a forger would not. Maybe it was John, the brother of James, the fisherman. Maybe it was someone else. Now would we know — without falling into safety or speculation?

It’s another one of those big problems — how would I know if I was wrong? — especially here, where I have strong engagements.

That’s why.

Matthew Brett
